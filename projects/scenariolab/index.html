The problem

Every student asks, “Why do I need to learn this?”, and most never find out. Knowing a subject isn't the same as knowing how to use it at work, where the real skill is the thought process: reading a brief, weighing trade-offs, designing a solution and explaining it so others can build it.

How it works

  1. Paste a lecture, syllabus or slides (PDF and PPTX are parsed on the server).
  2. The AI splits the material into tasks, and each becomes a realistic scenario with a role, stakes, constraints and a rubric, tailored to the student's career goal.
  3. The student designs the solution in words, as a drag-and-drop flowchart, or both. No code, so the time goes into thinking and explaining.
  4. An AI “senior” reviews the design against the rubric, with up to 3 rounds to improve before comparing against a suggested answer.
  5. Stuck? Ask for a hint, a simpler version, or chat with one of two mentors: Casey (kind) or Morgan (busy).

The AI is prompted to evaluate, not reveal: the reviewer points at gaps without giving the answer, and the mentor refuses to hand over the solution. Everything is stored in the browser, so there are no accounts and nothing to set up.

What's next

ScenarioLab fits computer science best today, where we know the professional workflow well. The same pattern of role, constraints, design, senior review and revision applies to any field with a professional practice, so the next step is researching how other fields actually work, so each subject's scenarios and feedback match real practice.
